Why provider directories go stale, and what keeps them current

By the Stethos team · November 4, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Directory errors come from many small, ordinary changes, not one big failure.
  • Waiting for providers to report changes guarantees lag.
  • Watching sources for changes beats re-verifying everything on a fixed schedule.

Anyone who has called a doctor's office from an insurance directory and reached the wrong practice knows the problem. Directories go stale, and they go stale for ordinary reasons.

Where the errors come from

  • Clinicians move. They join new groups, add a second location, cut back to part time or retire.
  • Groups change. Practices merge, get acquired by health systems, rename themselves and consolidate phone lines.
  • Networks change. Contracts start and end, and a practice can be in one plan's network and out of another's.
  • Sources disagree. The registry, the practice website, the hospital's site and each payer's directory may all say something different.

None of these is dramatic on its own. Together, across hundreds of thousands of clinicians, they add up to a directory that's never quite right.

Why reporting alone doesn't fix it

Most systems depend on providers telling them about changes. Busy practices don't always do that, and when they do, they tell one system, not every system. Federal rules now push health plans to verify directory information on a regular schedule, which helps, but a fixed schedule still leaves a gap between the change and the check.

What keeps a directory current

The teams that do this well flip the approach. Instead of re-checking everything on a calendar, they watch the sources that change first: practice websites, licensing boards, hospital rosters and job posts. When one of those sources moves, they re-verify that one record right away.

They also keep the evidence. A listing that says "verified at the practice website two days ago" is far more useful than one that just says "active." When sources conflict, they keep both and record which one won, so a reviewer, or an agent, can make the call.
