What the NPI registry can and can't tell you

By the Stethos team · November 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • The NPI is the one identifier almost every US provider record shares, so build on it.
  • NPPES is self-reported and only as current as the provider's last update.
  • Pair it with licensing, affiliation and network sources before an agent acts on it.

If you're building anything on US healthcare provider data, you'll meet the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System, or NPPES, on day one. It's the federal registry that issues National Provider Identifiers, and it's free to search and download. It's also easy to over-trust.

What an NPI is

An NPI is a 10-digit number that identifies a healthcare provider in standard transactions like claims. There are two kinds. Type 1 NPIs belong to individuals, such as physicians, nurse practitioners and therapists. Type 2 NPIs belong to organizations, such as hospitals, group practices, pharmacies and home health agencies. The number itself carries no meaning, and it's meant to stay with a provider for life.

That permanence is what makes it useful. Claims data, CRM records, credentialing files and payer directories can all be joined on the NPI, which is why it should be the key in your own data model too.

What the registry tells you well

  • Identity. Name, credential and whether the record is an individual or an organization.
  • Taxonomy. The provider's self-selected specialty codes, including a primary one.
  • Addresses. A mailing address and a primary practice location, with phone numbers.
  • Status. When the NPI was issued, last updated, or deactivated.

Where it falls short

The registry is self-reported. Providers are expected to update their information when it changes, but nothing forces the update to happen quickly, and many records lag behind reality. A physician who moved practices last year may still show the old address.

It also leaves out a lot of what commercial and clinical teams actually need:

  • Which hospitals and groups a clinician is affiliated with today.
  • Which state licenses are active, expired or restricted.
  • Which payer networks a practice participates in.
  • Every location where a clinician actually sees patients, beyond the primary one.

What to pair it with

Treat NPPES as the spine and attach other sources to it: state licensing boards for license status, CMS provider and facility data for affiliations and enrollment, payer directories for networks, and practice websites for where people work right now. Keep the source and date on every field you attach, so you can tell a fact checked yesterday from one that's three years old. That's the approach behind Stethos enrich, which returns a source and verified date with every field.

That last part matters most if software is going to act on the data. An agent can't ask a colleague whether an address looks right. It needs the provenance in the record.
